## Narration draft

Under the surface, a crocodile is responding to details we can barely see.

A crocodile's skin looks like armour. It is covered in scales and reinforced structures that protect the animal. But hidden within that protective surface are specialised sensory organs.

Scientists call them integumentary sensory organs, or ISOs. They are especially noticeable around the jaws. In crocodiles, they can also be found across the body. Their distribution differs between crocodilian groups, so it is important not to assume that every species has exactly the same pattern.

Research on Nile crocodiles and other crocodilians has shown that these organs contain networks of nerve endings and different types of receptors. Some respond to mechanical stimulation, including vibrations. That gives the animal a way to detect movement and contact through its skin.

One study examined the structure and response properties of these organs in juvenile American alligators and Nile crocodiles. The researchers recorded responses to different forms of mechanical stimulation, including vibrations in a range associated with water movement. The work supports the idea that crocodilian skin is much more sensitive than its armoured appearance might suggest.

A separate study compared sensory organs in Nile crocodiles and spectacled caimans. It found evidence that these tiny organs can respond to more than one kind of stimulus, including mechanical and thermal changes. The results are specific to the species and conditions studied; they should not be turned into a claim that every crocodile can detect every change in the water.

**[VISUAL REQUIREMENT: Film only genuine close-ups of the jaw and body scales. A macro shot must come from the original recording, not an AI reconstruction or an unrelated stock image.]**

The important point is not that a crocodile has a magical sixth sense. It is that its body is equipped with a specialised sensory system suited to life in and around water.

Think about what happens when something touches the surface. A moving animal, a falling object or a passing current can disturb the water. Some of those disturbances may be detected by sensory structures around the crocodile. The animal still has to interpret information in context. A scientific study showing a receptor response is not proof that a particular crocodile identified a particular object in the wild.

**[META AD BREAK — 02:30 — place after the explanation of the research and before the practical observation section.]**

When watching original footage, pay attention to the relationship between the animal and its surroundings. A crocodile may turn its head, adjust its position or move toward a disturbance. Those are visible actions. The reason for each action may not be visible, so narration must not invent an explanation.

This is what makes careful wildlife filmmaking so important. The camera records behaviour. Research helps us understand what may be happening. Good documentary storytelling connects the two without pretending that the footage proves more than it does.

A crocodile's skin is not simply a shield. It is also part of how the animal experiences the world.
