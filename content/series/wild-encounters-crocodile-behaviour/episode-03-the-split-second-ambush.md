## Narration draft

The strike is fast. The waiting is the real story.

A crocodile's hunt is not simply a chase. Much of the time, the animal is conserving movement, using the shape of the water and the shoreline, and waiting for an opportunity.

When a Nile crocodile swims, its powerful tail provides the main forward thrust. The body and tail move in a wave-like pattern, allowing the animal to move through the water. The legs can be held close to the body while swimming, reducing unnecessary movement.

Near the shoreline, a crocodile may keep much of its body underwater. Its eyes and nostrils can remain close to the surface, allowing it to observe while staying largely concealed. This is one reason a crocodile can be difficult to notice in the right conditions.

But not every crocodile that floats near the bank is hunting. A camera may record resting, thermoregulation, movement between locations or many other behaviours. A documentary must not turn every quiet moment into a hunt.

When prey capture does occur, the sequence can be rapid. A crocodile may accelerate from the water, seize an animal and use its strength to pull prey back toward the water. Larger prey may be handled in the water. These behaviours should be described only when the species, setting and actual footage support the narration.

Research on Nile crocodile feeding ecology also shows that diet can change with body size. A study in the Okavango Delta found differences in the kinds of prey consumed by different size classes. Smaller crocodiles relied more heavily on aquatic insects and arachnids, while larger size classes showed different patterns that included fish and a wider range of prey. These findings describe the population and methods studied; they are not a universal menu for every Nile crocodile.

**[VISUAL REQUIREMENT: Use a real, continuous sequence if a hunt was genuinely recorded. Do not splice unrelated footage to imply a single attack. If no complete hunt was filmed, build the episode around verified movement and feeding behaviour without pretending to show a strike.]**

The most important part of the story may be what happens before the attack. Positioning, patience and the ability to move efficiently through water all matter. The animal does not need to chase every opportunity. A missed opportunity is part of the natural process too.

**[META AD BREAK — 02:30 — place after the hunting mechanics and before the section on diet and size. Never cut into a strike, chase, bite or recovery sequence.]**

The difference between a strong wildlife documentary and a misleading one is often the edit. A dramatic shot can be real and still be presented dishonestly if it is taken out of context. Keep the original recording, preserve its sequence and make clear when narration is explaining general research rather than describing the exact filmed moment.

A crocodile's hunting strategy is shaped by its body, its habitat and the opportunity in front of it. The stillness is not wasted time. It is part of the strategy.

## Footage and editorial requirements

- No staged feeding, baiting, provocation or human interaction.
- Preserve continuous source footage for any claimed hunting sequence.
- Do not show graphic injury or prolonged suffering.
- Do not use unrelated prey footage as if it were captured with the crocodile.
- Identify the source location and species only where documented.
