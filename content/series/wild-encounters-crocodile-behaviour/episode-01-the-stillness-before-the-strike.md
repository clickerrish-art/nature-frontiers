## Narration draft

The river looks empty — until the surface moves.

At first, there may be almost nothing to notice. A line of water. A few ripples. A dark shape resting just below the surface. But a crocodile does not experience this scene in quite the same way we do.

To us, the water may look still. To a crocodile, small movements around the animal can carry information.

Researchers have studied tiny sensory structures in crocodilian skin called integumentary sensory organs. They are visible as small, often darker spots around the jaws. In crocodiles such as the Nile crocodile, similar structures are also distributed across the body scales.

These are not decorative markings. They contain sensory receptors that respond to mechanical stimulation. In laboratory research, some of these receptors responded to vibrations and very small movements. The findings help explain how crocodilians can detect disturbances in the water around them.

That does not mean every ripple tells a crocodile exactly what made it. It means the animal has a highly developed way of sensing its surroundings — even when much of its body is hidden.

Watch the way a crocodile holds itself in the water. The eyes and nostrils may remain near the surface while the rest of the body stays submerged. The animal can observe its surroundings without exposing much of itself.

The stillness is part of the story. A crocodile may remain in one position for a long time. But stillness should not be mistaken for a lack of awareness. The animal is receiving information through sight, hearing, smell and touch, while its sensory skin adds another layer to that picture.

The research is especially interesting because crocodilians combine tough, protective skin with remarkable mechanical sensitivity. The small sensory organs are one reason their armoured appearance can be misleading. The surface is protected, but it is not simply insensitive.

And this is where the opening image becomes more interesting. The river may look empty to us, but the crocodile is not waiting in a world without information. It is responding to a living environment.

When you review real footage, look for the details that are easy to miss: a slight change in posture, a small adjustment of the head, or a shift in position as the water changes around the animal. Describe only what the camera actually shows. Do not claim to know what the crocodile detected unless the behaviour is supported by evidence.

The first lesson of this series is simple: the crocodile's stillness is not the same as inactivity. It is an animal adapted to notice subtle changes in its environment.
