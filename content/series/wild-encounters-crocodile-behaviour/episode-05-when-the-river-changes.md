## Narration draft

When the water changes, the whole habitat changes with it.

A river is not a fixed backdrop. Water levels rise and fall. Channels shift. Vegetation changes the shape of the shoreline. And the animals living there respond to conditions that can vary from one season to the next.

Nile crocodiles use aquatic and nearby terrestrial habitats, but the way they use those spaces can differ with age and body size. Smaller animals may use different parts of a habitat from larger crocodiles. That can influence where they are observed and how they interact with their surroundings.

A study in the Lake Albert delta in East Africa examined habitat use across different size classes and seasonal activity patterns. The researchers reported evidence of habitat shifts between size classes and changes in observed activity across seasons. The study also emphasised that the reasons behind these patterns can be complex. Food availability, predation risk, competition and physical limits may all be relevant, but they should not be treated as a single proven explanation for every population.

This is an important distinction. A pattern recorded in one river system does not automatically describe every crocodile population across Africa. The local habitat, climate, prey community and human pressures all matter.

For a wildlife filmmaker, this creates an opportunity to tell a more complete story. Instead of presenting a crocodile as an isolated animal, show the habitat around it. Record the waterline, banks, vegetation and other visible features. Return to the same viewpoint when possible. A repeated camera position can reveal change without needing exaggerated narration.

**[META AD BREAK — 02:00 — place after the explanation of habitat-use research and before the field-observation section.]**

There is also a reason to be careful with the word “migration”. A crocodile moving from one part of a waterway to another is not automatically making a seasonal migration. Use that term only when a study or tracking data supports it.

The same caution applies to claims about drought. A falling water level may change where crocodiles are seen, but the response depends on the place and the conditions. Do not claim that a specific animal is moving because of drought unless the evidence supports that explanation.

The wider lesson is that behaviour cannot be separated from habitat. A crocodile's choices are shaped by the water, the shoreline and the other animals around it.
